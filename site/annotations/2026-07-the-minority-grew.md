title: The minority grew
date: 2026-07-30

The July call scored cleanly. Bank Rate held at 3.75% on 30 July; the locked probabilities returned a Brier of 0.003; nothing in the record moved between lock and score but the outcome field and the scores that follow from it. That is the mechanism working exactly as designed, and it is not what this note is about. The interesting thing about July is not the call. It is the part of the reading that failed while the call succeeded.

## 1. What happened

At the meeting ending 29 July, announced on the 30th, the MPC voted six to three to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%. Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill preferred an immediate quarter-point rise to 4.00%. It was the fifth consecutive meeting at 3.75% and the third consecutive meeting at which the hawkish minority has grown: 8–1 in April, 7–2 in June, 6–3 in July. The run begins from a unanimous 9–0 in March — the Committee's first undivided decision in four and a half years — which makes the sequence cleaner still: one dissent added at each of three successive meetings from a base of none.

The accompanying Monetary Policy Report carried a central projection of CPI peaking near 3.2% in the fourth quarter. At the press conference Bailey moved directly to damp the hawkish reading, telling reporters not to leave the room thinking the Bank was edging towards a hike. On the day, then, the vote record and the Governor's communication pointed in opposite directions, and the Governor was managing that divergence deliberately.

## 2. What I called

Hold at 3.75%. Correct. The probabilities were locked on 28 July under the announcement-minus-two convention, with p(hike) shaded modestly below the market-implied 3.86% for the two documented regime-specific reasons: a forward read at meeting-plus-three, interpolated across whole-month buckets on a steeply upward-sloping short curve, absorbs September's expectations into July's; and the fiscal repricing of 20 July that carried the ten-year gilt to 5.04% is a risk premium rather than an expectation of Bank Rate. Both remain testable against the September lock, when the near-month slope will differ. The communication index was recorded as context and kept out of the call, on the standing ground that L3 scores −0.6729 against the market-only benchmark and Spec 2 returns p = 0.4918. The ancillary vote-split call — unscored — was that the minority would hold at two. It went to three. Mann moved. She was named in the lock-day rationale as the member most likely to reason her way towards an activist hike while continuing to vote for a hold. I had identified the right person and drawn precisely the wrong conclusion about her.

## 3. Why I was wrong

The argument was that the macro flow since 18 June ran against the two dissenters on nearly every measure they had invoked. Headline CPI fell to 2.6% against a 2.7% consensus, its lowest in fifteen months; services eased to 3.6%; regular pay growth slowed to 3.4%, joint-slowest since October 2020; input producer prices decelerated to 7.3% from 9.3% as Brent retraced. Greene and Pill, on that reading, had dissented on an energy shock that was already unwinding.

The flow did run against them. Every one of those figures held. They dissented anyway, and a third member joined them.

So the error is not in the data. It is in the assumption that connected the data to the votes. I had read the June dissents as claims about realised inflation — that prices were running hotter than the majority allowed — and inferred that a run of disinflationary prints would erode the claim and, with it, the dissent. That is not what the dissenters were saying, and July made it explicit. Their stated reasons, taken together:

- Greene argued that moving early reduces the probability that second-round effects take hold at all.
- Mann named the decisive change as diplomatic and geopolitical — the collapse of the US–Iran memorandum, the widening of the conflict, the energy volatility that followed — and not any price series.
- Pill argued for raising now precisely in order to cut through the noise in commodity and asset prices, as an unambiguous signal of willingness to act on upside risk.

Not one of the three is an argument from realised inflation. Pill's is the sharpest against me: he treats the incoming data as noise to be seen through. My reasoning assigned decisive weight to a series that the decisive voters had explicitly set aside.

Bailey's own framing is the gap in a sentence. Inflation has fallen faster than expected, to 2.6%; the Bank's central projection has it peaking near 3.2% in Q4. Both are true at once. A committee looking backwards sees disinflation; a committee looking forwards sees a peak still ahead of it. The minority is not disputing the first fact. It is pricing the second. They are not reasoning from realised disinflation. They are reasoning from forward risk.

## 4. What it changes

Two things, of different orders.

The smaller is a correction to how I read the Committee. It is not divided about the object of concern. Bailey, Breeden, Lombardelli, Ramsden, Taylor and Dhingra all framed the hold in terms of second-round effects, insurance, or the conditions under which they would tighten; Ramsden said outright that crystallised upside risk could warrant a hike and that subsiding risk would have him considering a resumption of cuts. Everyone is watching the same thing. What separates the six from the three is the evidentiary threshold for acting on it before it appears. Greene would move pre-emptively; Ramsden would move on confirmation. On a hawk–dove scale those two positions read as near-neighbours. In vote space they are opposite.

The larger is that this may be a problem with what the index measures rather than with how I read its output. A lexicon keyed to realised-inflation language is not mis-calibrated here; it is oriented along the wrong axis. It cannot see the dimension the July vote actually turned on, and its failure mode is worse than silence: it will score July's disinflationary language as dovish at exactly the meeting where the hawkish minority grew. The index and the vote record will diverge systematically, and the divergence will present as noise rather than as the finding it is.

That points to a second series rather than a rescaling of the first — a risk-management measure counting conditionality, pre-emption, insurance, second-round effects, signalling and tail risk, scored separately from realised-inflation tone. The testable proposition is that the gap between the two carries the vote information: that in July the realised-inflation series fell while the risk-management series rose, and that the divergence, not the level of either, is what tracks dissent counts.

One discipline note, because it is the whole point of the exercise. None of this touches the July record, and none of it may be fitted to July. If a second series is built, it must be specified and pre-registered before the September lock and run over the full sample from January 2019 under the same expanding-window protocol as everything else. Otherwise the fix is in-sample, and the negative result it claims to overturn is merely reproduced with extra steps.

The last change I am persuaded of is procedural. The vote split should be pre-registered as a scored distribution from September rather than carried as an unscored ancillary. July's miss cost nothing in the ledger — which is precisely the problem. A reading that can be wrong without penalty is not doing the work the rest of the project does.
